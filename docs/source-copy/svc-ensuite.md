Ensuite Bathroom Renovations Sydney 
The bathroom you use most, designed for the room it sits inside. 
 
An ensuite is the bathroom you actually use the most. It is the first room you walk into in the 
morning and the last one you visit before bed. Renovating one is not the same as renovating 
a main bathroom, the brief is more personal, the privacy and acoustic considerations are 
higher, the ventilation problem is harder, and the design has to feel continuous with the 
bedroom it opens off. 
What we mean by an ensuite 
An ensuite is a bathroom accessed only from a private bedroom, almost always a master. It 
is distinct from a "main bathroom" (accessed from a hallway, used by the whole household) 
and a "powder room" (a small toilet-and-basin guest space).  
 
A Sydney ensuite typically serves one or two people regularly and ranges from 3 to 7 square 
metres in footprint. 
Five things that make an ensuite different to a main bathroom 
•​
Footprint. Most Sydney ensuites are 3–7 sqm. The same trades have to deliver the 
same quality of work in less than half the room. Per-square-metre cost is often higher 
than a main bathroom because the time on site does not scale linearly with size. 
•​
Ventilation. Most ensuites are internal — no external wall — so natural ventilation 
through a window is not available. Mechanical ventilation to AS 1668.2 is essential, 
and getting it right is harder than it looks. 
•​
Acoustic and privacy considerations. The ensuite sits inside the bedroom. Toilet 
noise, fan noise, plumbing noise — all of it travels into the room your partner is 
sleeping in. 
•​
Design continuity with the bedroom. The materials and palette should feel like they 
belong to the same room. An ensuite that fights the bedroom it sits inside feels 
institutional. 
•​
The shower-or-bath conversation. Ensuites typically skew shower-only. Households 
tend to keep a bath in the main bathroom and treat the ensuite as the daily-use 
space. 
Ventilation is the part most ensuite renovations get wrong 
Because most Sydney ensuites are internal, mechanical extraction is the only way to 
manage moisture. The National Construction Code Volume Two (Housing Provisions 
Standards) requires wet-area ventilation, and AS 1668.2 sets the technical performance.  
 
The consequences of getting it wrong are not cosmetic — they migrate into the master 
bedroom.

Typical signs of a poorly ventilated ensuite are paint peeling on bedroom skirtings, persistent 
damp smell, mould on bedroom walls adjoining the ensuite wall, and warped timber finishes 
inside the bedroom itself. Some Sydney homes are also losing mattress life to the moisture 
migrating overnight. 
 
We design ensuite ventilation around three things: 
•​
Fan capacity matched to room volume and required air-change rate per AS 1668.2 
•​
Duct routing — short, straight runs to external air, ideally not running through 
bedrooms 
•​
Run-on timers and humidity sensors so the fan extracts after the shower stops, not 
while you are still in there 
Acoustic and privacy detailing 
An ensuite is in a different acoustic position to a main bathroom — what happens in one 
room is heard in the other. We treat acoustics as a design item, not an afterthought: 
•​
Solid-core doors rather than hollow-core, with acoustic seals on the frame and 
threshold 
•​
Concealed cistern toilets (in-wall) — quieter than close-coupled, and visually quieter 
too 
•​
Position of the toilet pan relative to the bedhead — we keep them on opposite walls 
where possible 
•​
Soft-close drawer and door hardware on all joinery 
•​
Insulated walls between ensuite and bedroom where the build allows 
Walk-in robe adjacency, when an ensuite project becomes a 
master-suite project 
Many Sydney ensuites share a wall with a walk-in robe. Renovating both together rather 
than separately opens up real design options: relocating a vanity into the WIR-side of the 
wall, sharing a wet wall, integrating a vanity-and-dressing-table sequence. If you are 
renovating an ensuite and the WIR is tired, doing them together is usually the cheaper 
option, not the more expensive one. 
Typical timeline and budget 
Sydney ensuite renovations typically run: 
•​
Refresh (same layout, new finishes): 1–2 weeks · $8,000–$18,000 
•​
Full renovation (same footprint, new everything): 3–4 weeks · $20,000–$40,000 
•​
Reconfigure or WIR-adjacent expansion: 4–6 weeks · $40,000+ 
 
Small does not always mean cheap.  
 
The same number of trades has to fit into the room over the same number of days, and the 
higher specification of an ensuite (concealed cistern, niche, mirror cabinet, heated rail, 
premium finishes) often drives the per-square-metre cost above that of a comparable main 
bathroom. 
How we work, what we build to 
Every Elite Touch ensuite is delivered to the same compliance framework as a full bathroom: 
•​
Wet-area waterproofing to AS 3740-2021, certified 
•​
Plumbing and drainage to AS/NZS 3500 by a NSW-licensed plumber 
•​
Electrical to AS/NZS 3000 Wiring Rules, including IP-rated fittings where wet-area 
zoning requires them 
•​
Mechanical ventilation designed to AS 1668.2 and NCC Volume Two

Frequently asked questions 
How small can an ensuite be in Sydney? 
Functionally, a one-person ensuite can fit into around 2.5 sqm with careful planning (shower, 
basin, WC). Most lived-in ensuites are 3–7 sqm. Below 2.5 sqm the room starts to feel like a 
powder room with a shower bolted on, and the design loses some of what makes an ensuite 
an ensuite. 
 
Can I add an ensuite to a bedroom that does not have one? 
Often yes, depending on whether a walk-in robe or wall can be sacrificed and how plumbing 
can be routed to and from the new room. We do a feasibility check at the on-site consultation 
— it is rarely an outright no, but the build complexity and cost depend heavily on plumbing 
access. 
 
Do ensuites need natural light? 
No, but they benefit from it. Most Sydney ensuites are internal and rely on mechanical 
ventilation plus electric lighting. A skylight, where the roof geometry allows, is the easiest 
way to bring daylight into an internal ensuite without the privacy compromise of a window. 
 
Should I include a bath in my ensuite? 
For most households, no. Most ensuites are too small to carry a bath without compromising 
the rest of the room, and households tend to use the bath in the main bathroom anyway. 
Larger ensuites (8 sqm+) can accommodate a freestanding bath if the brief calls for it. 
 
What floor tile is right for an ensuite? 
A floor tile with a slip-resistance classification of R10 or higher (per AS 4586) is the safe 
default for a domestic ensuite. Smaller-format tiles (300×300 mm or mosaics) are easier to 
fall to a floor waste than large-format tiles, which require flatter substrates. The choice is 
design-led but constrained by the build. 
 
How loud are ensuite extractor fans? 
Good ones are around 0.6–1.0 sones at quiet settings — quieter than the bedroom ceiling 
fan in most homes. We specify fans by both extraction rate (litres per second) and noise 
(sones), because a fan that does not stay on after the shower is a fan that has not done its 
job.
